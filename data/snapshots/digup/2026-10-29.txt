 What is DigUp? 
 A search app for the files on your computer. It runs Google DeepMind's EmbeddingGemma 2, a model that puts text, pictures, sound and video in one shared space, on your own machine. You describe what you remember, and it finds the photo, the PDF page, the minute in a recording or the moment in a video. It's free and open source, on a Mac, on Windows and on Linux.
 How is it different from Spotlight or Windows Search? 
 They find files by their names, text and metadata. DigUp also finds them by what they show or say, and takes you to the page or moment that matched. They work fine side by side.
 What does DigUp send over the network? 
 Nothing you index leaves your computer. DigUp goes online for two things: to download the model once, from Hugging Face, and to check for updates once a day, on GitHub. The update check sends nothing about your files, and you can turn it off in Settings → General. No account, no telemetry. Privacy and security
 has the details.
 Why is the download 865 MB? 
 That's the model: the text part (310 MB) and the image and audio encoders (555 MB). It downloads once. The app itself is about 20 MB on a Mac, and 40 to 140 MB on Windows and Linux, depending on the package, since those carry their own PDF, video and image readers.
 Will it slow my computer down? 
 Only the first pass is heavy, and it happens once. After that DigUp reads only what's new or changed. The model runs in a separate helper that quits when it's done, and the search side only loads while you search. On a Mac, Low Power Mode and a hot Mac make the first pass wait, and Index only at set hours can keep it to the night.
 Which languages does it understand? 
 The model knows more than 100 languages, and DigUp searches in all of them, across languages too: a search in English can find a note in Bengali or Arabic. DigUp's tests cover English, Bengali and Arabic because those are the languages of the files it was tested on, and Google notes that the model isn't equally strong in every language. Two limits apply to exact words, not to meaning: the text inside screenshots is read only on a Mac, by Apple's text recognition (about 25 languages), and languages written without spaces, like Chinese, Japanese and Thai, aren't split into words.
